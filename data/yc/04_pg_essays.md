# Key Paul Graham essays (summaries)

Paul Graham (PG) is YC's cofounder and the author of dozens of essays that have
become canonical reading in the startup world. Below are summaries of the most
influential ones for founders.

## "Do Things That Don't Scale" (July 2013)
The most-cited PG essay. Argues that successful startups typically begin with
**manual, unscalable work**: recruiting users one by one, doing concierge
onboarding, hand-delivering the product. Examples include Airbnb's founders
flying to New York to photograph hosts' apartments, and Stripe (then
"/dev/payments") personally installing their API on the first customers'
laptops. The thesis: the early-stage bottleneck is *learning*, not scale.

## "How to Get Startup Ideas" (November 2012)
Best startup ideas are **noticed, not invented**. They come from finding
something missing in your own life. PG warns against "made-up" ideas that
sound plausible but solve no real problem. The litmus test: are you a member
of the market? Do you have a "schlep" — an unpleasant problem you'd pay to
have solved?

## "Default Alive or Default Dead?" (October 2015)
Every founder should know, at any given moment, whether their company is
**default alive** (will become profitable on current burn before money runs
out) or **default dead** (will not). The math is simple: take current monthly
revenue, project its growth, and see if it crosses current monthly expenses
before the bank account hits zero. Most founders are too optimistic; PG
recommends doing the calculation explicitly.

## "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" (July 2009)
Engineers and writers operate on a **maker's schedule** (half-day blocks of
deep work) while executives operate on a **manager's schedule** (hour-long
meetings). A single meeting can blow up a maker's whole afternoon. The
implication: protect makers' time, batch meetings at the ends of the day.

## "The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn" (April 2006)
Eight rules. The hardest one: **release early**. Most founders polish too long.
Other rules include: keep pumping out features, make users happy, fear the
right things, commitment is a self-fulfilling prophecy, there is always room
for new entrants, don't get demoralized, speak the user's language.

## "How to Start a Startup" (March 2005)
The foundational essay that led to YC's existence. The three things a startup
needs: **good people, something customers actually want, and to spend as
little money as possible**. Everything else is secondary.

## "Startup = Growth" (September 2012)
Defines a startup as **a company designed to grow fast**. Newly founded ≠
startup; small ≠ startup; tech ≠ startup. The defining characteristic is
intentional, rapid growth. Healthy weekly growth rates are 5–7%; great ones
are 10%+; sustained 1% weekly growth is the floor for being on the path.
